Post

AI agents can write code, research a market and draft a contract. What they still can't do is pay for something, or get paid for it.

That sounds like a small gap. I think it's the main reason most agents are still demos.

Picture an agent that needs a dataset, a translation or a person to check its work. Today it stops and waits for a human to create an account somewhere, type in a card number, approve an invoice and paste the result back. The agent needed four seconds. The admin takes two days.

It's the same the other way round. If you've built an agent that does one job well, selling that job means building a checkout, writing terms and chasing people for payment. Most builders stop there.

That's the gap vAPI Network is built for. An agent should be able to find a service, pay for it in digital dollars and get the result back without anyone logging into a dashboard. When the job is bigger than one request, the money should sit in escrow until the work is done.

The part I find hardest is liability. If an agent buys the wrong thing, who pays for the mistake? If it hires a person and the work is poor, who decides? Nobody has fully answered that yet, us included.

If you build agents: what's the first thing you'd want yours to be able to buy on its own?

Post

If an AI agent does a job wrong, who gives the money back?

We spent [PERIODE INVULLEN] on that one question while building vAPI Network. This is where we landed, and what we still haven't solved.

Every marketplace eventually hits this moment. The work is delivered, the client says it isn't what they asked for, and the seller says it is. With agents it gets stranger, because the seller might be software that can't explain itself.

On most platforms the platform decides. That's a quiet conflict of interest, because the platform needs both sides to come back.

We chose something else. The payment sits in escrow from the start. If there's a dispute, three independent reviewers check the work against the scope both sides signed. When two of them agree, that's the outcome: pay out, refund or split.

We went with three because one reviewer is a single point of failure and two can deadlock. Three gives a majority without turning every disagreement into a court case.

It only works because the scope is signed before the work starts. The reviewers aren't judging who seems more reasonable. They're comparing the work with a document.

What we haven't solved: how a reviewer judges output that's technically correct but useless. If you've hired or built agents, how would you define "wrong"?

Unpopular opinion: most "AI agent marketplaces" are directories.

A directory helps you find an agent. It doesn't help you pay one safely, and that's the part that makes a marketplace.

Look at what Upwork gets paid for. It isn't the list of freelancers. You can find freelancers anywhere. People pay the fee because the money for a fixed-price job sits in escrow and there's a process when things go wrong.

Agent directories mostly skip that part. You find an agent, then you sort out payment and trust yourself. For a quick experiment that's fine. For work a business depends on, it isn't.

A decision we made early: freelancers on vAPI Network get paid in digital dollars (USDC), not in their local currency.

I want to explain why, including the downside.

The upside is speed and cost. A USDC payment arrives in about a minute and costs a few cents. An international bank transfer can take two to five working days, with fees on both ends and a currency spread on top. For small jobs, those fees eat a big share of what you earn.

Escrow also works better this way. The money can sit in escrow and move the moment the work is approved, without waiting for a bank to open on Monday.

The downside: if your rent is in euros, pesos or rupees, at some point you have to convert. That step has a cost, and we'd rather say so upfront than hide it. [HOE OMZETTEN NAAR LOKALE VALUTA WERKT: INVULLEN]

Stablecoins are also regulated now, under the GENIUS Act in the US and MiCA in the EU. That doesn't make them right for everyone, but it does mean the risk is no longer the one people remember from a few years ago.
